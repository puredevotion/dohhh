Cow-meat. Chinese does not have a separate word for beef the way English does — it names the animal and the substance, and lets you do the arithmetic.

One character learned, four dishes readable. 牛 turns up again in 牛奶 milk and 牛排 steak.

CAN YOU SEE THE ANIMAL?

In three of them, yes — these are pictures, drawn three thousand years ago and worn smooth:

牛
ox head, seen head-on. Two horns, then the face.
羊
the same head, horns curving down. A goat.
鱼
head, body, tail. The tail flattened into one stroke.

In the others, no — and it is worth knowing where the picture stops. There is no duck in 鸭. What there is, is a bird, plus a character borrowed only for its sound:

鸭 甲 jiǎ — there for the sound only  +  鸟 bird
猪 者 zhě — sound only  +  犭 just “four-legged animal”. No pig in there at all — though the traditional 豬 keeps one, 豕.

Squint at 牛 and you are seeing what the scribe saw. Squint at 鸭 and you are inventing something. The honest version is also the more useful one: 鸟 means bird wherever it appears, so it opens 鸡 chicken, 鸭 duck, 鹅 goose and 鸽 pigeon at once. A made-up duck opens one character.

肉 TRAVELS IN DISGUISE

Squeezed into the left of another character, 肉 flattens into 月. Once you see it, the organ section of a menu stops being a lottery:

肝liver 肠intestine 肚tripe 腰kidney 脑brain

A hint, not a rule — the same 月 is also the moon, as in 月票 monthly pass. But on a menu it is nearly always flesh, and that is the one place you need it.
